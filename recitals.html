How it works

Nobody is forced on stage, and nobody is left off it.

Students who want to play, play. Their teacher helps pick a piece that will sound good on the day and works it with them in the weeks before.

Emma Hewitt organizes both recitals. If you want to know when the next one falls, or whether your student is ready for it, ask her teacher at the next lesson.

Beginners are welcome. A first recital is often thirty seconds long, and thirty seconds in front of an audience is worth a month of practice alone in a bedroom.

Getting ready

How a piece turns into a performance.

The work happens in the ordinary weekly lessons. There is no separate rehearsal to sign up for.

  1. The teacher picks the piece

    Something the student can already mostly play, chosen to sound good in a room rather than to stretch them. A stretch piece is for the lesson, not the stage.

  2. It gets polished, not learned

    The weeks before are spent on the parts that fall apart under pressure — the start, the ending, and the one bar that always trips.

  3. They play it standing up, from memory or not

    Sheet music on a stand is fine. The point is playing for people, not proving a memory.

  4. Then they do it again in six months

    The second one is where you see the difference. The nerves are the same and the playing is not.
